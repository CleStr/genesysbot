Abstract
This report, part of the gEneSys project (WP2), presents an intersectional analysis of energy marginalization in the EU and Sub-Saharan Africa, identifying six vulnerable groups to inform future energy policy development. Using survey data (EU: n=18,465; Sub-Saharan Africa: n=11,536), we employed intersectional clustering to identify groups facing systematic exclusion from energy systems due to limited financial, social, and cultural resources. Key indices, including the Energy Capability Index (ECI) subdimensions (detailed in Annex: Energy Capability Index Subdimensions), were analyzed to assess how energy systems condition citizens’ substantive opportunities towards energy justice with particular attention to political agency, experiences of energy poverty, and opportunities to act environmentally friendly. . Detailed personas were developed for each group, integrating demographics, energy use, and marginalization factors, to humanize their challenges and support policy reflection. The report provides data-driven insights through statistical summaries and narrative descriptions, enabling experts to design targeted energy policies addressing issues like high energy poverty, low political self-efficacy, and eco-anxiety. This work, funded by Horizon Europe (No. 101094326), lays the foundation for equitable energy transitions without directly proposing policy recommendations.

Executive Summary
This report presents political recommendations for action based on key findings from the gEneSys project, focusing on energy marginalization in the EU and Sub-Saharan Africa. Energy marginalization is defined as the systematic exclusion from energy systems due to limited access to financial, social, and cultural resources, leading to high energy poverty, low political self-efficacy, and reduced sustainability capabilities. Drawing from analyses in our previous D2.2 and D2.3 reports, we identify three vulnerable groups per region (six total) that repeatedly experience such marginalization.
Using statistical data from project surveys (EU: n=18,465; Sub-Saharan Africa: n=11,536), we modelled these groups with intersectional attributes and calculated persona data, including primary energy types and mean index values (e.g., Energy Capability Index - ECI subdimensions). The ECI subdimensions range from 1 to 7 or 1 to 5, assessing aspects like energy management, barriers, access, health impacts, decision-making, and political engagement, with higher values indicating better capabilities (except for Energy Barriers and Health Impacts, where higher values indicate greater challenges).
Personas were developed to humanize these groups, providing one-page descriptions with demographics, energy use, marginalization factors, and representative images. Experts can use these for reflection and developing measures.
Recommendations emphasize targeted policies like subsidies for renewables, education on energy efficiency, and community empowerment to reduce marginalization. This report's methodology ensures data-driven, transparent insights for EU and Sub-Saharan policymakers.
Introduction
The gEneSys project investigates energy marginalization, where vulnerable populations face barriers to equitable energy access and participation. Based on energy marginalization indicators and intersectional analyses, this D2.5 report identifies six vulnerable groups and provides personas to inform political recommendations. Groups were selected based on consistently low scores in indices specifically developed to capture the complex impacts of the energy system on citizens. 
The report structure includes:
Methodology: Group identification, modelling, and persona compilation.
Results: Persona descriptions and index summaries.
Methodology
Group Identification
Group identification was carried out with a particular focus on vulnerable populations, whose position was examined through their scores across a range of variables and composite indices developed within the study (see Reports D2.2 and D2.3 for details). We employed a combination of distinct variables and newly constructed indices, structuring the analysis in two stages. In the first step, a set of diverse indices included in the survey – each comprising multiple items (see Annex 8.xxx) - was selected. These indices capture different directions and are intended to represent various manifestations of vulnerability in the energy context.

For the second stage of the analysis, the Capability Approach, following Martha Nussbaum (2000), was operationalized. The aim was to provide a holistic representation of the complex impacts of the energy system on citizens. A range of survey items was developed for this purpose - some newly created, others adapted from existing questionnaire constructs. These items were subsequently validated, resulting in the creation of the Energy Capability Index, structured around a six-dimensional framework. The six factors, each comprising multiple survey items (see Annex 8.3), are presented below.
2. Energy Capability Index (ECI)
Energy Management and Environmental Choices (measures the ability to optimize energy use and make eco-friendly decisions)
Energy Barriers to Daily Life (measures the impact of energy costs and availability on daily activities)
Energy Access and Safety (measures the reliability and safety of energy supplies)
Health Impacts of Energy (measures physical and mental health effects from energy issues)
Energy Decision-Making (measures the ability to make informed energy choices)
Political Engagement on Energy (measures the ability of involvement in energy policy discussions)
The identified vulnerable groups showed repeated disadvantages across these, justified by lack of capital (financial, social, cultural) and structural factors (e.g., gender inequality in EU, infrastructure gaps in Sub-Saharan Africa).

To understand how vulnerability and disadvantage are distributed across different segments of the population, we examined key demographic identity variables. This allowed us to capture intersecting social positions and explore how multiple forms of identity shape experiences of energy-related risks and capabilities. Intersectionality was assessed by examining demographic identity variables, including relationship status (QP21: 1=Single, 5=Divorced/Separated, 6=Widowed), education level (edu_lvl: 1=Low/Primary or less), gender (QS1: 1=Women), age (resp_age: continuous, years), ethnic minority status (QP12: 1=Yes), care responsibilities (QG0), urbanity (QP28: 1-7 scale, 1=rural to 7=very urban), household size (QP24: continuous, number of inhabitants).
Group Formation Process
The groups were selected through a multi-step process: First, we analyzed the D2.2 and D2.3 reports using a large language model (LLM) to identify groups performing consistently low on  our indices created (see above). This generated an initial list of potential vulnerable groups. We then double-checked this list manually against the raw data and reports, followed by team discussions to refine the selection based on vulnerability criteria such as intersectional disadvantages and repeated marginalization patterns. In the end, the group selection was a discursive, interpretative process grounded in the D2.2 and D2.3 data analyses, ensuring alignment with project goals of addressing energy injustice.

Vulnerable groups personas
EU Countries – 
Single individuals with low education (especially women) –
Persona description is as follows, Maria is a single woman in her early fifties, residing in a moderately urban area in an EU country such as Poland or Italy. With only primary education or less, she is often retired or living on a pension, though some in her group engage in part-time or full-time work. Her income, primarily from wages or social transfers like pensions, feels strained, making it challenging to live comfortably. She lives alone in a modest rented flat, managing her household independently without the support of a partner or family.
Maria’s health is fair, neither particularly good nor poor, and she lives with a chronic illness, such as joint pain or respiratory issues, that has persisted for months. While she does not consider herself to have a disability, mental health diagnoses like depression or anxiety are common among her group, though she personally focuses more on her physical well-being. She identifies with a Christian worldview, though some in her group hold no specific beliefs or identify as atheist or agnostic.
In her flat, Maria primarily uses an electric stove for cooking meals, tea, or boiling water, and relies on central heating powered by piped natural gas to warm her home. Her lighting and overall electricity needs are met through a national grid connection, with occasional use of liquefied petroleum gas or wood for specific purposes. She does not own or co-own any renewable energy installations, such as solar panels, and depends entirely on traditional energy sources, reflecting limited access to sustainable alternatives.
Her efforts to save energy, such as turning off appliances or managing heating, are slightly above average, showing a moderate commitment to energy conservation despite financial and educational constraints. Her capability for energy-efficient and sustainable behaviors aligns closely with the EU average, suggesting she makes reasonable efforts to adopt such practices when possible. However, environmental concerns cause her less stress than the average EU resident, as her focus remains on immediate needs like health and household expenses. Energy Capability Index Subdimensions
The following tables provide detailed breakdowns of group characteristics and ECI subdimensions for transparency and further reference.
The Energy Capability Index (ECI) comprises six subdimensions, each derived from survey items tested for cultural relevance and reliability. The table below summarizes each subdimension, its definition, and corresponding survey items. 
Dimension
Definition
Item Formulations
Factor 1: Energy Management and Environmental Choices
This part measures how well people can control their energy use to save resources and protect the environment. It looks at whether they can use energy efficiently, understand energy policies, reduce waste, and choose eco-friendly options like renewable energy or green products. This reflects their ability to make choices that support sustainability and climate goals, which is important for both personal and global well-being.
- QE1_11: Are you able to optimize and manage your energy consumption in the most efficient way possible? - QE1_12: Do you feel competent about energy policies and regulations that may impact your energy choices? - QE1_13: Are you able to avoid unnecessary waste of energy in your household? - QE1_14: Are you able to engage in environmental and climate protection? - QE1_15: Are you able to choose to use renewable energy sources (for cooking, heating, vehicle’s fuel, etc.)? - QE1_16: Are you able to choose environmentally friendly alternatives when you make purchasing decisions?
Factor 2: Energy Barriers to Daily Life
This part checks how much energy costs or shortages get in the way of everyday activities. It asks if a lack of affordable or available energy stops people from working, studying, feeling safe, staying connected online, or enjoying hobbies like watching TV or listening to music. These barriers can lower quality of life, so this sub-dimension highlights how energy access affects daily routines and well-being.
- QE1_7: Does affordability or availability of energy impact your ability to study or work at home? - QE1_8: Does affordability or availability of energy impact your ability to have visitors at your home? - QE1_9: Does affordability or availability of energy impact your sense of safety and security in your home? - QE1_19: Does affordability or availability of energy impact your ability to access online and digital communication services such as websites, messaging, and phone calls? - QE1_20: Does affordability or availability of energy impact your ability to enjoy recreational activities (such as TV, radio, and music) and hobbies in your home?
Factor 3: Energy Access and Safety
This part measures whether people have enough energy for their homes and if it’s safe to use. It looks at affordability, availability, reliability (no power outages), and health risks from energy use, like pollution or unsafe equipment. It also checks if people can move around without fear of energy-related dangers, like electrical shocks or gas leaks. This sub-dimension is key to ensuring energy supports a safe and comfortable life.
- QD11_1: I can financially afford the amount of energy and energy resources required to supply all areas of my household. - QD11_2: Energy and energy resources are available to me in the amounts needed to supply my household. - QD11_3: My household’s energy and energy resources supply are reliable without outages. - QD11_4: The energy use in my household does not cause any harm or risks to my health (including the sourcing of materials and energy generation). - QE1_4: Are you able to move from one place to another without fear of energy-related hazards (such as electrical shocks, gas leaks, fires caused by faulty wiring, and other similar dangers)?
Factor 4: Health Impacts of Energy
This part focuses on how energy problems, like high costs or poor energy services in the area, affect people’s health. It asks if struggles to afford energy or local energy issues (e.g., pollution or unreliable supply) make people feel sick or stressed. This sub-dimension is important because health is a core part of well-being, and energy access can directly influence it.
- QE1_2: Is your health impacted by affordability or availability of your energy use? - QE1_3: Is your health impacted by the energy situation in your area?
Factor 5: Energy Decision-Making
This part measures how well people can understand energy issues and make choices that match their values. It looks at whether they can make informed decisions about energy use, form opinions about energy sources and policies, and choose energy options that align with their beliefs, like saving energy or supporting green energy. This reflects their ability to take control of their energy use and contribute to energy discussions.
- QE1_5: Are you able to make informed decisions about your energy use? - QE1_6: Are you able to form an opinion about energy issues such as energy sources available and how energy is produced or distributed? - QE1_10: Are you able to make conscious choices about your energy use in line with your values?
Factor 6: Political Engagement on Energy
This part checks if people feel that politicians listen to them about energy concerns. It asks whether leaders stay in touch with ordinary people and care about their thoughts on energy issues, like prices or renewable energy policies. This sub-dimension shows how much people trust that their voices matter in shaping energy decisions, which is part of energy justice.
- QA2_1: Politicians strive to keep in close touch with the people regarding energy issues. - QA2_2: Politicians care about what ordinary people think about energy issues.

Conclusion
This report advances the gEneSys project's mission to address energy marginalization by conducting a comprehensive intersectional analysis of vulnerable groups in the EU and Sub-Saharan Africa. Through rigorous methodology, including survey data from over 30,000 respondents, group formation via SPSS syntax, and persona compilation based on key indices like the Energy Capability Index (ECI) subdimensions, it identifies six marginalized groups characterized by intersecting factors such as gender, education, age, ethnicity, care responsibilities, and urbanity. The developed personas—Maria, Aisha, Elena for the EU, and Amina, Zainab, Fatuma for Sub-Saharan Africa—humanize these groups, detailing their demographics, energy use patterns, health challenges, and socio-economic vulnerabilities, while highlighting implications for support programs to enhance energy access, sustainability, and well-being.
By providing data-driven insights into energy poverty, low political self-efficacy, and eco-anxiety, this work lays a solid foundation for policymakers and experts to craft targeted interventions, such as subsidies for renewables, education on efficiency, and community empowerment initiatives.
